Case Comp Deck Designer

Builds, critiques and learns from case-competition decks. It follows a case comp playbook and a pattern library distilled from nine reference decks.

Use it
  1. Copy the skill with the button above.
  2. Paste it into a chat with Claude and ask it to save it as a skill.
  3. Attach your case and ask for a deck.

Prompts to try once it is saved

  • Build a 6-slide submission deck for this case. The problem statement and judging criteria are attached.
  • Critique my draft deck against the rubric and give me the top fixes.
  • Learn from these decks and update the pattern library. The second one won its competition.

Built from one 51-slide playbook session and nine reference decks (134 slides). The library holds layout and structure patterns only, not any team's analysis or numbers.

Design decks for case competitions (B-school case comps, live consulting challenges, corporate pitch rounds) so they read like the decks that reach finals: one argument, proven slide by slide, at consulting density.

Two things drive every decision here:

  • The playbook (Build mode below): how to decode a brief, build the argument and lay out the deck. It comes from the user's "Case Competition 101" session.
  • The pattern library (end of this file): what the user's reference decks actually look like, with one card per deck. When the library and a general default disagree, the library wins, because it is the user's taste written down.

Who the deck is for

A judge screening many submissions, giving each deck a minute or two, often with no presenter in the room. Judges reward sellability: an idea clear enough to understand, credible enough to trust and sharp enough to remember. It breaks into five tests, and every slide should help at least one:

Test The judge is asking
Clarity Can I follow the logic fast?
Feasibility Would this survive real constraints: cost, owners, time?
Innovation Is the idea distinctive and useful?
Evidence Are the numbers and assumptions defensible?
Storytelling Does the deck build belief slide by slide?

The clearest, most feasible, most sellable idea wins, not the cleverest one. When a choice comes up between showing more analysis and making the recommendation easier to believe, choose belief.

Build mode

1. Decode the brief

A deck that answers the wrong question loses however good it looks, so pin these five down in writing before anything else. Read what the user attached first, then ask only for what is missing.

  1. Core objective: the one outcome the organiser wants ("grow B2B e-commerce using Gen AI").
  2. Context versus constraints: what is given about the situation, and what limits the answer (budget, adoption inertia, regulation).
  3. Deliverables: exactly what was asked for (a solution, a GTM plan, 5-year financials). Each one needs a slide.
  4. Scope: market, geography, time frame, what is out.
  5. Unknowns: what the brief does not say and research or assumptions must fill.

Also get the rules that change the deck: slide limit and whether the appendix counts, judging criteria and weights, file format, whether it will be presented or only read, and the client's brand. If the user is not around, assume a read-only 16:9 PDF, state the assumptions at the top of the reply and carry on.

How the brief is written changes how much to assume:

Brief type Handle it by
Detailed, lots of data Selecting. Use the three or four facts that drive the answer and leave the rest out.
Minimalist Stating assumptions early and visibly.
Hidden identity Working out who the company is from the clues and verifying with public data.
Fictional Anchoring every idea in a real parallel.
Hybrid Separating real facts from invented conditions on the slide.
Open innovation Defining the problem yourself before solving it. That definition is slide one.

The kind of case decides which proof the judges expect to see:

Case type Proof that has to be on the slides
Strategy Market entry or growth logic, options weighed, portfolio choices
Marketing Segmentation and targeting, positioning, funnel, channel budget with CAC and ROI
B-plan TAM, SAM, SOM, business model, unit economics
Operations Where the bottleneck is, cost or time saved, process before and after
Social / ESG Stakeholder adoption, measurable impact, cost per outcome, sustainability
Product / open innovation Problem definition, user journey, product mockup, implementation logic
Finance Scenarios, sensitivity, risk quantified, hedging or funding plan

2. Pick the deck format

The reference decks come in two shapes (eight are reading decks, one is a live finale deck), and mixing them up is costly, so decide before writing slides.

Submission deck (read without you) Live finale deck (presented)
Length 5-8 content slides plus appendix 25-35 slides
Words per content slide 250-400, in 3-5 labelled sections 30-120 on most slides
Each slide A complete mini-argument with its proof One message or one big number, built up over several slides
Devices Section tracker, tables, charts, outcome boxes Persona story, big-number tiles, product demo, QR code
Dense slides All of them Only feasibility, risk and cost, mostly in the appendix

Default to the submission deck unless the user says it will be presented live.

3. Build the argument

Lead with the answer. Write the pyramid before any slide:

  • The question the judges asked.
  • The answer in one decision-ready sentence: what, for whom, how and by when.
  • Three reasons, mutually exclusive and together complete (for example customer pull, financial viability, execution readiness).
  • Evidence under each reason that survives a follow-up question.

Test it by reading only the top line of each box, top to bottom. If that still argues the case, the pyramid holds.

Turn data into insight. Every number on a slide should complete this chain, or it is decoration:

  • Data: "Of every Rs 100 spent, Rs 46 goes to the salesman."
  • Insight: "Sales cost is the biggest piece of the spend."
  • So what: "A tech-enabled ordering channel attacks the largest cost line."

Structure the problem. Name the north-star metric, then split what holds it back into branches with no overlap and no gap (revenue, cost, customer, execution is a common first cut). Test which branch matters most and spend the deck there.

Prioritise. Nobody can solve everything, and judges value depth over breadth. Place the candidate ideas on impact against feasibility, keep the top two or three, and put the detailed scoring (impact, cost, time, scalability, risk, differentiation) in the appendix.

Make it a solution tree. North star, then two or three levers, then initiatives under each. An initiative earns a place on a slide only when it has an owner, a timeline, a cost and a KPI.

Use frameworks as tools. A framework belongs on a slide only if it changes a decision or produces a number. Use it to organise the thinking and let the audience barely notice it. A slide titled with the framework's name is usually a sign it is filler.

4. Lay out the spine

The winning spine has six parts. Under a tight limit each is one slide; with more room, the solution and the selling plan get more.

  1. Problem and insight: the tension and the one finding that reframes it
  2. Solution overview: the answer and its two or three levers
  3. How to sell the solution: go-to-market, adoption, how it gets past the market's resistance
  4. Financials and ROI: what it costs, what it returns, when it pays back
  5. Roadmap: phases, owners, milestones
  6. Q&A readiness: risks and mitigation in the deck, backup in the appendix

Then:

  • Write the ghost deck: slide titles only, in order. Read top to bottom they should be the whole argument. Think of each slide as an episode that leaves the reader wanting the next one.
  • Map slides to the judging criteria. A criterion worth 30% with no slide behind it is the most common way good analysis loses.
  • Show the user the ghost deck before building when they are present. Changing a title costs nothing; changing a built slide costs a rebuild.

For a presented deck, the opening two minutes follow a fixed order: one line of context and tension, one sharp insight or surprising number, the recommendation in one sentence, then how the deck will prove it.

5. Design each slide

One message, dense proof. The playbook says one idea per slide and the reference decks carry 300 words a slide. Both are true: each slide makes one claim in its title, and everything below it is organised evidence for that claim. Density comes from tables, charts and labelled blocks, never from paragraphs. If a slide has two messages, split it.

Anatomy of a content slide in a submission deck:

  • Header band in the client's brand colour with the title in it, client logo at the right.
  • Action title: a full sentence stating the conclusion, two lines at most, with a number where one exists. "Chennai resists quick commerce but offers a first-mover advantage", not "Chennai market analysis".
  • Section tracker across the top when the deck has four or more sections, with the current one filled.
  • 3-5 sections, each headed by a filled label bar. The eye should travel left to right through a complete story: what is wrong, what we do, what it achieves.
  • Proof beside the claim: the table, chart or calculation sits next to the conclusion it supports.
  • Outcome box at the end of each solution block: the quantified result, visually distinct ("about 80% of time spent on inventory saved").
  • Source footnotes, numbered, in small type, and a slide number.

A solution slide should answer these in the order the eye meets them: what the product looks like (a mockup or minimum viable version), how it fits into the user's day, what impact it makes, which pain point it addresses, what value it adds, and why it is practically feasible.

Pick the archetype from what the slide has to prove. All of these appear in the reference decks:

The slide has to show Archetype
The whole answer on one page Current state, then gaps, then thesis and success metrics
The market and why now Stat strip of 4-5 big numbers above primary research and desk research side by side
That real customers feel the problem Quotes from named personas, survey size stated, field photos

How we compare 2x2 positioning map, or a feature matrix with ticks and crosses and competitor logos

6. Apply the design system

Use the library's design system at the end of this file. If the user names a client or brand, take the palette from that brand. The reference decks do this consistently: the deck looks like it belongs to the company it is written for.

7. Prove it numerically

Numbers turn a pitch from aspirational to defensible. The financial slide should cover as many of these as the case allows: unit economics (price minus variable cost), investment needed split into one-time and recurring, break-even (how and when cost is recovered), payback period, ROI, and a multi-year view tied to the roadmap. Replace adjectives with rupees, percentages, time or volume everywhere, and show units, scope, time frame and source for each figure.

Every number needs a source or the label "assumption". Never invent data to fill a chart. When a figure is missing, estimate it with visible logic and label it an estimate, or leave a marked placeholder and tell the user. Judges probe numbers first, and one unsupported figure discredits the rest.

8. Prepare the appendix

Judges have unspoken doubts in a predictable order. Have backup for each, answer-first and easy to find:

  1. Assumptions and data quality: source table with dates, sensitivity
  2. Unit economics and hidden costs: cost build-up, buffers, exclusions
  3. Feasibility and execution: owners, dependencies
  4. Alternatives and trade-offs: why this option over the others
  5. Risk, compliance and ethics, including any use of AI

When space is short, keep three: assumptions, sensitivity, risk mitigation. The reference decks keep a clearly marked appendix after the main story, and three of them link or QR-code out to the working model.

9. Build it

Build the deck with whatever slide-building tool or skill this session offers, and follow that tool's own instructions for the mechanics. Produce a .pptx when the user asks for PowerPoint. Confirm the deck can be exported in the format the competition wants.

10. Check before handing over

Render the finished deck and look at every slide. Score it on the rubric below and fix anything under 5 that can be fixed.

Criterion 1 3 5
Clarity Hard to follow Mostly clear Crisp and answer-first
Feasibility Unclear execution Some plan Cost, owners and timeline clear
Evidence Claims only Some backing Numbers and sources
Story Disconnected Logical sequence Memorable narrative
Q&A readiness Weak backup Some answers Appendix-ready

Then run the seven ways good ideas lose:

  1. Text overload: paragraphs where a visual or callout would do
  2. Solving the wrong ask: recheck objective, constraints and deliverables against the brief
  3. Weak feasibility: missing owners, timeline, costs or dependencies
  4. No quantification: no rupees, percentages, time or adoption range
  5. Framework dumping: frameworks that do not drive a decision
  6. No risks: top risks and mitigation absent
  7. Unprepared Q&A: no appendix

And the mechanics: slide count within the limit, every deliverable and judging criterion has a slide, no overflow or overlap, nothing below 8pt, numbers agree across slides, arithmetic re-run. Tell the user plainly about anything that did not pass and any placeholders left in.

Critique and redesign mode

Read the draft the way a reference deck is read (images plus numbers). Then:

  1. Write the ghost deck the draft actually has. Topic-only titles show up immediately.
  2. Score it on the rubric and the seven pitfalls. Give the user the three or four changes that matter most first, not a list of twenty.
  3. If asked to redesign, keep the user's analysis and numbers, restructure the storyline on the spine, and rebuild the slides. Say what was cut and why.

Learn mode

Turn each new reference deck into a deck card, then update the cross-deck patterns.

  1. Look at every slide as an image. Layout, hierarchy and density are visual facts that extracted text hides. The script below renders the pages.
  2. Get the numbers from the same script.
  3. Write a deck card using the template in the library.
  4. Update the cross-deck patterns. Promote something to a pattern only when it appears in two or more decks, or the user says "this is the one I want to match". If the user says how a deck placed, record it and weight winners more.
  5. Persist it. The library lives in this file, so propose an updated version of this whole skill with the library rewritten and everything else unchanged, for the user to save. If this session cannot update a skill, give the user the updated library as a document to attach next time.

Take structure, archetypes, layout, density and visual devices. Do not take another team's analysis, wording, numbers or branding.

# usage: python3 read_deck.py deck.pdf out_dir     (needs pdfplumber and poppler's pdftoppm)
import sys, os, statistics, subprocess
import pdfplumber

src, out = sys.argv[1], sys.argv[2]
os.makedirs(out, exist_ok=True)
subprocess.run(["pdftoppm", "-scale-to-x", "1280", "-scale-to-y", "-1", "-jpeg", src, os.path.join(out, "s")], check=True)
with pdfplumber.open(src) as pdf:
    rows = []
    for n, page in enumerate(pdf.pages, 1):
        words = page.extract_words(extra_attrs=["size"])
        pts = [w["size"] * 960 / float(page.width) for w in words]   # as if on a 13.33in-wide slide
        rows.append((n, len(words), len(page.images), min(pts, default=0), statistics.median(pts) if pts else 0))
content = [r for r in rows if r[1] >= 60]                             # skips covers, dividers, thank-you
print(f"{len(rows)} slides, {len(content)} content slides, median {statistics.median(r[1] for r in content):.0f} words per content slide")
for n, w, imgs, lo, med in rows:
    print(f"{n:>2} | {w:>4} words | {imgs:>2} images | body {med:4.1f}pt | smallest {lo:4.1f}pt")

Convert a .pptx to PDF first and run the same script. Read titles, colours and layout from the rendered images; the script only counts.

Pattern library

Learned from the "Case Competition 101" session deck and nine reference decks. Placements were not given for any deck, so none is weighted as a winner; two are national-finale decks and are marked.

Design system

  • Canvas: 16:9 in all nine decks.
  • Background: white or near-white in six of nine. Dark or textured backgrounds appear in three and cost legibility in each.
  • Palette: borrowed from the client. One dominant brand colour for the header band and label bars, one accent for highlights and outcomes, greys for containers. Seen: blue with yellow for an e-commerce client, black with orange for an EV brand, maroon for a state government, brown for a coffee exporter, purple for a consulting firm's own competition.
  • Type: one sans family per deck (Arial, Calibri, Poppins and Noto Sans all appear). Title 18-20pt bold, label bars 11-13pt bold, body 10-12pt, tables 9-10pt, sources 8pt. The reference decks often drop to 5-7pt inside tables and it cannot be read; hold 8pt as the floor and move the overflow to the appendix.
  • Header: full-width band in the brand colour carrying the title, optionally a one-line subtitle under it, client logo top-right.
  • Section tracker: a row of tabs or chevrons across the very top naming the deck's 5-6 sections, current one filled. Three of the nine decks and the worked example use it.
  • Label bars: every block on a slide is headed by a filled bar or chip with a short label.
  • Containers: thin outlines or light tints. Dashed outlines mark outcomes and emphasis.
  • Emphasis: bold the key phrase inside a sentence; colour only the number or the chosen option.
  • Small devices: icons beside labels, competitor and partner logos in place of their names, numbered circles for steps, arrows and chevrons to show flow between blocks.
  • Footer: tagline or logo bottom-left, numbered source footnotes, slide number.

Cross-deck patterns

  • Arc: landscape and problem, who the customer is, the strategy in one framework, solutions, how to sell them, financials, roadmap, risks, thank you, appendix.
  • Density: eight of nine are dense reading decks. Content slides run 180-450 words, median about 300, in 3-5 labelled sections, with body text at 9-12pt.
  • Name the idea. Almost every deck gives its solution or campaign a brand name and uses it throughout. A named idea is easier to remember and refer back to.
  • Primary research is shown, not mentioned: survey size, quotes attributed to named personas, field photos, even video links. Three decks and the worked example do this, and it reads as effort and credibility.
  • A persona carries the story in two decks, from the problem slide to the outcome slide.
  • Targets are written as from and to: "CAC Rs 3,500 to Rs 1,800", "attendance 68% to 80-85%".
  • Solutions come in threes, each with its own what, how and quantified outcome.
  • Product cases show the product: mockups or sample screens in five decks.
  • Financials: an investment figure in the title or a headline tile, a unit-economics table or waterfall, a 3-5 year or 3-phase projection, break-even or payback stated.
  • Roadmap: three phases or years, each with focus, partners or owners, and expected outcome.
  • Risk: a risk and mitigation table in five decks.
  • Close: a thank-you slide with team names and photos, and a marked appendix holding model tables, assumptions, survey data and links to working files.
  • Titles: only two decks write full-sentence titles throughout; the rest mostly use topic labels. The playbook asks for the headline to be the insight, so write sentence titles.

Do not copy: neon colours on black; text under 8pt; spreadsheets pasted in as images; topic-only titles; a one-slide business model canvas at 800 words; background photos behind text; one slide stretched to cover four topics.
